# Yes, Your Code Is Opinionated

![Untitled](Yes%2C%20Your%20Code%20Is%20Opinionated/Untitled.png)

- The "*un-opinionated*" vs "*opinionated*" code argument is an argument that does not make too much sense. There is a problem in the way libraries are presenting themselves as "*un-opinionated*".
- All the libraries you use in a daily basis are very opinionated. They are flexible, bendable and reconfigurable but they still hold opinions. What these tools are opinionated about can vary, but none of them are "*un-opinionated*".
- Code is given shape by the opinion of the writer. Code is an expression of our opinions.
- Your opinion expands on everything you code: How a function should be named, how a method should have defined the public API, how a module should interact with another, etc. The style guide you follow is still another opinion.
- One of your jobs as a developer is to consider the next person that is going to deal with your code, which is why that the author has found that it is best to make those opinions known through documentation and examples.
- Writing "*Getting Started*" guides and "*How To*" articles is better than just writing API documentation alone. Make your opinions explicit so other developers who follow behind will be able to understand your implementation.
- When you force yourself to make an implicit opinion explicit, you are required to examine your beliefs which is highly difficult because you can be wrong.
- If you can accept that your opinions can be wrong when tested against the opinion and experiences of others, they will hold much power and weight.
- Be open to the possibility to change your opinion in the future.
- Some useful questions:
    - “*Would the team benefit from your opinions being explicit?*".
    - “*Would you benefit from this piece of code in the future?*".
    - “*What are the beliefs of this person when they wrote this piece of code?*".
